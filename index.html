Home Improvement ROI in 2026: Which Projects Pay Back — and Which Don't

Not every renovation dollar comes back at resale, and in 2026 the gap between the best and worst projects is enormous. Exterior projects pay back best: a garage door replacement recoups about 268 percent of its cost, a steel entry door about 216 percent, and manufactured stone veneer about 208 percent, per the latest Cost vs. Value Report. Those are the rare projects that return more than they cost — mostly because they are cheap relative to the curb appeal they add.

A minor kitchen remodel recoups about 113 percent, which makes it one of the few interior projects that can pay for itself. The key word is minor: new cabinet fronts or a reface, new counters, new appliances, fresh paint — the room looks new without moving a single wall. A major upscale kitchen, by contrast, recoups only about 36 percent. The extra $80,000 of premium finishes and layout changes buys enjoyment, not resale value, and buyers rarely pay for someone else's taste in stone and appliances.

That split is the whole lesson. The projects with the highest return are the ones buyers can see from the street and the ones that fix an obvious problem: a dented garage door, a drafty entry door, a tired facade. The projects with the lowest return are the ones that are personal, expensive, and invisible to the next owner — luxury bathrooms, high-end kitchens, and anything that only you will ever notice.

There is an important caution on the numbers themselves: there is no 2026 edition of the Cost vs. Value Report yet, so the percentages above come from the latest published edition and should be treated as planning guidance, not a guarantee. Markets move, and a project that pays back in a hot market may not in a slow one. Regional differences matter too — the same remodel can return far more in a high-cost coastal market than in a market where buyers expect lower prices.

Resale ROI is also not the same as enjoyment value. A bathroom you use every morning for ten years is worth doing even if it returns half its cost at sale. The right question is not "does it pay back?" but "does it pay back enough for what it costs me, given how long I will enjoy it?" A $13,000 budget on a $400,000 home, spent on the highest-return items, beats a $50,000 spend on the lowest-return ones — every time.
